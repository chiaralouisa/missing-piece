Finding 01 · affects the headline

A model that never looks at the patient scores 0.749

In a simulated cohort built so that the target genes are statistically independent of the patient — zero recoverable signal, by construction — the prevalence-only baseline still reaches a pooled AUROC of 0.749 — the same order as the range the abstract reports. Pooled AUROC is dominated by differences in prevalence between genes, not by anything learned about a tumour. Per-gene (macro) AUROC, which is prevalence-free, correctly reads 0.500 on the same predictions.

The ten findings

None of this says the result is wrong. Several findings say the number as reported cannot distinguish a real result from a null one — a different problem, and a fixable one. Each finding names the fix.

01
Could invalidate the headline

The reported AUROC may not measure what the conclusion claims

With 164 genes at 1.21% prevalence, the value of AUROC depends entirely on how it is averaged, and the abstract does not say. A gene altered in 12% of tumours contributes mostly-positive pairs; one altered in 0.3% contributes mostly-negative ones. Rank all pairs together and gene prevalence does the work.

Note too that under macro AUROC the claim “exceeded a prevalence-only baseline” becomes near-vacuous: that baseline is 0.500 by construction, so any signal at all exceeds it. The informative comparison is finding 02.

Fix. Report macro (per-gene) AUROC as the headline. Keep pooled AUROC only beside the prevalence-only baseline computed identically, and beside its prevalence-adjusted twin.

02
Decides the interpretation

The baseline that matters is mutational burden, and it is missing

Two very different mechanisms produce cross-panel predictability. Gene-specific co-alteration — EGFR-mutant tumours look unlike KRAS-mutant ones — is the interesting claim. Mutational burden is not: a hypermutated tumour has more alterations everywhere, so counting altered genes on the 341-gene panel predicts counts on the other 164 with no gene-specific information at all.

Burden alone clears a prevalence-only baseline comfortably. So the stated comparison cannot separate “the panel predicts the genome” from “burden predicts burden” — and only the first supports the conclusion.

Fix. Add a burden-only baseline: one logistic regression per target gene whose sole input is the number of altered genes on the observed panel. Report macro AUROC minus burden as the primary effect size.

03
Cohort construction

Unassayed genes are missing data, not wild-type

A patient sequenced with IMPACT341 was never interrogated for the 164 IMPACT505-only genes. Scoring those as “no alteration” adds 164 guaranteed negatives per patient and inflates every metric.

MSK-CHORD is ~25,000 tumours and its NSCLC subset is far larger than 2,226 — if your cohort came from restricting to IMPACT505-sequenced patients, this is already handled. Say so explicitly; a reviewer will ask.

One consequence for framing: restricting that way makes the “IMPACT341 input” a retrospective mask on IMPACT505 data, not real small-panel output. That is the right way to build the benchmark, but it is not the deployment setting, and the claim of broadening access rests on transfer across that gap.

04
Cohort construction

Repeat samples per patient leak across the split

MSK-IMPACT cohorts contain multiple samples from the same patient. Two samples from one tumour share most of their alterations; if one lands in train and the other in test, the model is scored partly on memorisation. The abstract says “held-out patients”, which suggests this was handled — make it explicit.

05
Reporting

The split percentages sum to 105%

“75% … 15% for further validation … the last 15% of held-out patients.” Presumably 70/15/15. Trivial to fix, but it is exactly the kind of thing a reviewer notices, and it costs credibility on everything else.

06
Statistical power

A 15% test split cannot support a 164-gene macro average

334 test patients × 1.21% prevalence is about four expected positives per gene. Most of the 164 genes have too few positives for a per-gene AUROC to mean anything, and the macro average then silently describes only the handful of common genes.

Measured: with a single 15% holdout, ~50 of 164 genes were scoreable at a five-positive threshold. Under 5-fold cross-validation with pooled out-of-fold predictions, 138 of 164 were.

Fix. Use repeated K-fold CV on out-of-fold predictions, and always report how many genes entered the macro average.

07
Implementation trap

Pooling out-of-fold predictions introduces a bias of its own

Found while building this pipeline, and worth knowing before you switch to CV. A gene's training prevalence is estimated excluding the fold it is applied to, so a fold holding unusually many positives was trained on a cohort with correspondingly fewer, and receives systematically lower scores. Pool the folds and the ranking runs backwards: the prevalence baseline, which must score exactly 0.500, came out at 0.424, and every model inherited the same downward bias.

A related trap bit twice. Rank metrics have no resolution floor — AUROC ranks a one-ULP difference as confidently as a difference of 0.5. Centring a constant column leaves per-gene residues of ~1e-16, which then get ranked, silently restoring the prevalence ordering the centring existed to remove.

Check. If you implement fold pooling or prevalence adjustment yourself, verify your prevalence baseline returns exactly 0.500. If it does not, this is why.

08
Framing

Nothing in the evaluation yet rewards the model for being generative

Per-gene AUROC scores marginals, one gene at a time — precisely what a discriminative multi-label classifier optimises. On this metric it is the ceiling, not the comparison. Measured under 5-fold CV, both discriminative baselines beat both flow-matching variants.

That is not an argument against the generative framing; it is an argument that the framing needs a metric reflecting it. What a generative model uniquely gives you is a joint distribution: coherent whole profiles preserving co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity, and calibrated uncertainty.

Fix. Report joint-structure metrics alongside AUROC — fidelity of sampled gene–gene co-alteration, and distance between sampled and true per-patient burden distributions. Independent draws from a discriminative model's marginals are the right null.

09
Method refinement

How you read probabilities out of a flow model changes the AUROC

Gaussian flow matching produces samples, not probabilities. Estimating a marginal means integrating many trajectories and averaging, so resolution is bounded by the sample count — with 64 samples nothing finer than 1/64 exists, ties proliferate, and AUROC pays for it. That is an artifact of the readout, easily mistaken for the method underperforming.

Discrete flow matching on {0, 1, MASK} avoids it entirely: at t = 0 every gene is masked, so one forward pass returns exact conditional marginals with no sampling noise, while ancestral unmasking still gives joint samples. For sparse binary genomics it is the better estimator and about 2× cheaper.

Worth checking. If the 0.77–0.79 spread came from “variants of architecture and training paradigm”, some of it may be readout variance rather than genuine architectural difference.

10
Clinical claim

AUROC is not a clinical claim

AUROC is threshold-free and prevalence-blind; neither property survives contact with a clinic, where a clinician acts at one threshold on a population with a specific base rate. At 1.2% prevalence a gene with AUROC 0.80 can still have a positive predictive value in the low single digits at any usable sensitivity.

  • Report PPV and sensitivity at real operating points, per gene, plus net benefit.
  • Report calibration — a probability informing a decision has to be right, not merely well-ordered.
  • Restrict a secondary analysis to actionable genes; a mean over 164 genes of mixed relevance tells an oncologist nothing to act on.
  • Correct for multiple testing across 164 genes.
  • Frame the output as triage — “this tumour warrants reflex broad sequencing” — never as a substitute for assaying the gene.
